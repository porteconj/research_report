Why does heart disease weigh more on some Portuguese adults than on others? A new study finds the answer is closely tied to income and, above all, education. In Portugal, cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death, and related conditions such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease are common. Yet national figures say little about who carries this burden. Using the 2019 National Health Survey, the paper examines how seven conditions are distributed by income and education among nearly 11,000 adults aged 35 to 79.

Every condition is more common among the less advantaged. Adults in the poorest fifth percentile reported heart attacks, strokes, diabetes and kidney disease two to two-and-a-half times as often as the richest fifth. The education gap was wider still: nearly six in ten adults with no completed schooling reported high blood pressure, against about one in six among master's-level graduates. For diabetes, the figures were one in four against one in twenty. These inequalities hold after accounting for age and sex, and education is consistently more strongly linked to ill health than income.

Why would this happen when the National Health Service is open to everyone? Care is largely free at the point of use, but medicines might weigh on household budgets, and limited access to family doctors hampers early detection and management of chronic conditions. Education may also matter through health literacy: the ability to understand medical advice, navigate the system, and adopt healthier habits.

Universal coverage per se does not guarantee equal cardiovascular health. The survey records self-reported conditions at a single point in time, although similar trends were described in earlier versions of this survey. Still, the findings point to prevention and care strategies that consider patients' socioeconomic status, not only clinical risk.
