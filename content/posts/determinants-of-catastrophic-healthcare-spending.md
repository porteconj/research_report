How effectively does Portugal’s universal healthcare system protect households from financial hardship when they need care? This study examines catastrophic health expenditure in Portugal and finds that, although financial protection has improved substantially over the past two decades, important inequalities remain. The incidence of catastrophic health expenditure fell from 9.42% of households in 2005 to 5.04% in 2022. However, this improvement was highly uneven: in 2022, catastrophic health expenditure affected 18.17% of households in the lowest consumption quintile, compared with just 0.63% in the highest.

Portugal’s National Health Service (NHS) is the cornerstone of the country’s healthcare system. Beyond providing access to healthcare services, it is also intended to protect all residents from the financial consequences of poor health through universal coverage, broad benefit entitlements, and public financing. Nevertheless, households continue to make significant out-of-pocket payments for healthcare, particularly for medicines and other services requiring copayments. While these payments represent about 30% of total healthcare spending on average, their financial impact varies greatly across households depending on income and healthcare needs.

Using the World Health Organization’s concept of catastrophic health expenditure, this study assesses the extent to which healthcare payments reduce households’ ability to meet essential living expenses and how this has evolved since 2005. The results show that Portugal continues to experience relatively high levels of catastrophic health expenditure by international standards, despite improvements following the public-finance crisis of 2011–2014. The analysis also documents substantial disparities across income groups and identifies the main categories of spending responsible for financial hardship. Medicines remain a particularly important source of burden for lower-income households, even though average spending per medicine has fallen or remained stable over time.

These findings suggest that the NHS does not yet fully achieve its financial protection role. Strengthening protection against catastrophic health expenditure should therefore remain a central policy objective.
