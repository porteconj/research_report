Can a shortage of male workers bring women into physically demanding jobs that conservative societies reserve for men? Women’s progress into male-dominated occupations is often attributed to changing attitudes. Portugal in the 1960s offers a different angle. Social norms were deeply traditional, but men suddenly became scarce. The Colonial War and mass emigration drove more than a million people, mostly men, out of the country.

This paper compares birth cohorts and regions that lost very different shares of their men. Where men became scarcer, women’s share in physically demanding, traditionally male occupations was higher by 1981. A 10 percent drop in the number of men per woman raised women’s share in these jobs by 4 to 5 percentage points.

Second, the paper looks at how this change happened in the salt ponds of Alcochete, near Lisbon, then Portugal’s largest salt-producing center. In the early 1960s, the salt workers there were all men. The job involved carrying 50-kilogram baskets of salt on one’s head, a task few then considered suitable for women. As emigration and new factory jobs drew men away, the largest employer began hiring women. By 1971, women made up one-fourth to one-third of its peak-season workforce.

This shift coincided with the spread of a simple technology: the wheelbarrow. It was first used in a single salt pond in 1954 and spread across the region from the mid-1960s. With it, one worker could move three times as much salt, which made physical strength less of an obstacle to hiring women. 

T﻿hese findings suggest that labour shortages can lead employers to do more than hire women. They can also lead employers to adopt technologies that make men and women more interchangeable at work, even where social norms resist.
