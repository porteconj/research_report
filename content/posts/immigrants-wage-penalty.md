Immigration debates often focus on whether newcomers have the skills, education, and language abilities to succeed in their new country. But there is another important question: how much do employers know about the people they are hiring?

When employers have little experience with a particular group of immigrants, they may be uncertain about workers’ productivity. They may also hold negative perceptions shaped by limited contact. Either way, immigrants can face disadvantages before they have a chance to demonstrate their abilities.

This research examines what happens when employers are suddenly exposed to large numbers of immigrants. Portugal provides a revealing case. After receiving relatively little immigration until the late 1990s, the country experienced a rapid influx of newcomers, including people from regions with few historical or cultural ties to Portugal.

Using matched employer–employee administrative data, the paper tracks the wages immigrants receive when they first enter the Portuguese labor market. This approach allows the study of how outcomes change across successive arrival cohorts without confusing employers’ growing experience with immigrants’ own gains in language skills or work experience.

T﻿he paper finds three main patterns. First, the initial wage gap between immigrants and native workers shrinks for successive cohorts. Second, wage differences among immigrants with similar observable characteristics decline, suggesting that employers’ assessments become more consistent as groups become established. Third, employers with more experience hiring immigrants tend to offer newcomers higher wages and exhibit less variation in their pay. These patterns are particularly strong when previous immigrant hires remain longer, suggesting positive employer experiences matter.

Experience also appears to travel with people. When human-resources managers move between establishments, their prior experience hiring immigrants predicts increased immigrant hiring at their new workplaces.

Together, these findings suggest that employer exposure can contribute substantially to immigrant integration.  The paper estimates that cumulative exposure over two decades may have eliminated up to 46 percent of the initial wage gap between native workers and newly arriving immigrants in Portugal.

Immigrant integration is not solely about how quickly newcomers adapt. It also depends on how quickly employers learn. Creating opportunities for that learning may be an important, often overlooked, way to narrow wage gaps.
