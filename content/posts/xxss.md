Why did Portugal fall behind comparable advanced economies after the 1974 Carnation Revolution, with a persistent gap? 

![The vertical dashed line marks 1974 (Carnation Revolution). The synthetic series is constructed using the method of Abadie et al. (2010) to match Portugal’s pre-1974 characteristics. ](https://ucarecdn.com/a172dd7c-6065-4d91-a4a8-a36e6b87cce6/ "GDP per capita for Portugal and its synthetic control")

Recent research shows that the Revolution marked a significant break in Portugal’s economic trajectory. As the figure illustrates, Portuguese GDP per capita tracked a synthetic counterfactual closely before 1974, but subsequently diverged, yielding a large and persistent gap. This paper studies the determinants sustaining and expanding this divergence.

This paper focuses on the role of structural reforms. Using a new annual dataset covering labor-market regulation, unemployment benefits, collective bargaining, minimum wages and product-market regulation from 1970 to 2025, the paper traces changes in Portugal’s institutional framework and its relation to the growth gap.

The results reveal an asymmetry between product-market deregulation and labor-market deregulation. Product-market deregulation is associated with a gradual narrowing of the gap, while greater labor-market rigidities are associated with persistent divergence, particularly over longer horizons. The main channel appears to be investment: institutional arrangements that make adjustment more difficult can weaken firms’ incentives to invest and thereby affect capital accumulation over time. Employment and productivity play more limited roles.

The effects also depend on the broader economic environment. They become stronger when Portugal faces external imbalances: under tighter external constraints, rigid institutions are associated with larger costs, while reforms that facilitate adjustment become more valuable.

Overall, the paper suggests that the effects of deregulation are not unambiguous. Rather, institutional design matters, as do timing and economic circumstances. Portugal’s experience suggests that political and economic shocks can have very long-lasting consequences when the institutions that emerge afterwards affect investment and the economy’s capacity to adjust.
