What happens to public finances when a country contracts immigration flow? The question is central to political debate across Europe including Portugal where immigration flows have risen sharply and restrictions are under discussion. 

T﻿he results show that holding euro area immigration flow at its 2019 level, covering the costs of aging would require a permanent tax increase of 14%. Closing the borders pushes that to 16.3% — an extra burden of about 1 percent of GDP a year. In Portugal, the same calculation gives almost 15% under 2019 flows and 20% with no immigration, the largest gap in the euro area.

T﻿he results hold even though immigrants are typically low-skilled. They arrive as young adults, after the expensive years of childhood in their native countries, and begin contributing at once. So although immigrants pay less over a full lifetime than natives by being low-skilled, someone arriving by age 25 is a smaller burden than a child born today. T﻿he same argument explains why encouraging births is a weaker remedy to alleviate public finances, and a far slower one. A newborn pays nothing for two decades, so relief arrives long after the pressure from aging has set in. Raising fertility to replacement rates delivers about a third of what immigration provides.

Not all immigration counts equally. Part of this is scale: in countries where inflows are already high, admitting more adds little, while where flows are small, each additional arrival matters more. Read in reverse, this means the first cuts to immigration are cheap and the last are expensive. But the differences across the euro area also reflect how much immigrants contribute, which depends on their wages relative to natives, their age at arrival, and each country's tax and benefit system. A uniform European migration policy is therefore hard to justify. This is why Portugal's gap is the widest: it is the only country in our data where immigrants contribute more to the public accounts than natives do, and so has most to lose from closing the door.
