Containing pharmaceutical spending is a major challenge for governments. Policies that reduce drug prices can generate important savings for patients and public budgets, but they can also reduce the revenues to pharmacies and other providers. A less visible question is whether pharmacy workers ultimately bear part of these costs through lower wages and by how much. 

T﻿his paper studies this question using a series of reforms implemented in Portugal between 2010 and 2014 that substantially reduced the revenues of community pharmacies. The reforms lowered regulated drug prices and pharmacy margins and encouraged greater use of generic medicines. Over this period, real value added per pharmacist fell by roughly one third. T﻿he paper finds that much of this shock was passed on to pharmacists, but the burden was distributed very unevenly. A new collective bargaining agreement introduced in 2012 established substantially lower wage floors for newly hired pharmacists and slower wage progression. As a result, new hires earned around 40% less than incumbent pharmacists. By 2018, more than 60% of pharmacists were covered by this lower wage schedule, accounting for about 88% of the decline in average wages between 2009 and 2018.  

Remarkably, lower wages were not accompanied by falling employment. The number of pharmacists working in community pharmacies continued to grow by close to 3% per year, increasing by at least 35% between 2009 and 2022. The supply of new graduates also remained sufficiently large to sustain employment growth, although pharmacy programmes have gradually become less attractive to prospective university students.  

The Portuguese experience shows that workers may absorb a substantial share of the adjustment in price-regulated industries, with particularly persistent consequences for new generations entering the profession.
