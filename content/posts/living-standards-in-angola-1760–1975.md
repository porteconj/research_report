How poor were ordinary Africans under colonial rule, and how much did coerced labor keep them poor? Recent research has challenged the idea that Africa was always the world’s poorest region. Portugal ran one of Europe’s longest-lasting empires, yet its African colonies remain barely studied.

T﻿he paper tracks workers in Luanda and Benguela, Angola’s oldest colonial cities, from 1760 to independence in 1975, using nearly 12,000 wage records and 1,200 price entries. For each decade, the authors count how many “baskets” a day’s pay bought, for free unskilled workers, each holding one adult’s bare daily minimum of food, fuel and other basics.

Until about 1820, buying power rose and Angolan laborers lived at least as well as Beijing’s workers. It then slipped, falling further after Portugal banned slave exports in 1836 and captives once shipped abroad were put to work locally. For the rest of the century, a non-coerced laborer’s full-time pay could barely, if at all, keep a family of four at the minimum. Coerced workers, being the majority, were poorer still: a day’s pay bought them under two baskets before 1900 and never more than four before independence, against roughly seven to eighteen in colonial Ghana. Non-coerced Angolans only reached in 1930 what Ghanaian and Nigerian laborers could afford in 1880.

From 1899, a law obliging Africans to work kept wages low, bar brief gains, until coerced labor ended in the early 1960s. Non-coerced workers’ living standards then more than doubled within a decade, with no fall in birth rates to explain it. The few skilled African craftsmen fared better, living before 1900 about as well as their peers in Florence and Milan. Coercion and meager colonial investment help explain why Angolans stayed poorer than workers in British West Africa or Dutch Java.
